\section{Introduction}

Gregory Clark's \emph{To Have and Have Not} \citep{Clark2026} builds its case
chapter by chapter, and the book's own summary of that progression (p.~11) is
worth following closely. Chapter 2 argues that twin and adoption studies have
systematically overestimated the role of social causation because they
implicitly assume zero genetic correlation between parents; once realistic
assortative mating is allowed for, little independent social causation
remains to explain. Chapter 3 introduces the book's two evidentiary pillars:
the Families of England database, linking 436,000 individuals across four
centuries of rare-surname lineages out to fourth cousins, and the Marriages
of England database, covering 1.7 million marriage certificates from 1837 to
2022. Chapter 4 is the empirical core: across outcomes including literacy,
higher education, occupational status, house value, local social environment,
and company directorships, the pattern of correlations among English
relatives from 1600 to 2026 --- even out to fourth cousins --- is shown to be
consistent with \citet{Fisher1918}'s model of additive genetic transmission
combined with strong assortative mating (reported $R^2$ above 0.97), with the
resulting intergenerational persistence essentially unchanged across four
centuries of social upheaval. Chapter 5 extends the same model to Denmark and
Sweden, finding similar parameters in recent educational-attainment data and
concluding that these ostensibly high-mobility Nordic welfare states are, in
fact, no more mobile than England is now or was before 1800. Chapter 6 turns
to ``genetic nurture'' --- the possibility that genes act indirectly through
the childhood environment they shape --- and argues that recent attempts to
demonstrate its importance fail, and that twin and adoption studies would
already have detected it if it mattered much. From this foundation the book
goes on, in later chapters, to draw out implications for education spending,
immigration selection, and differential fertility.

\section{The Fisher/Goldberger Critique}

The book's central empirical exercise is to fit \citet{Fisher1918}'s classical
model of phenotypic assortative mating to the observed pattern of status
correlations among relatives. It is worth being precise about what that model
assumes, what it implies, and what those implications cost the author, because
the costs are severe enough to undercut the book's central causal claim on its
own terms --- using only the numbers Clark himself reports.

Before turning to those costs, it is worth saying why a full derivation is
the right way to proceed at all, rather than simply reporting that the model
fits. A model's moment conditions are only as trustworthy as the assumptions
used to derive them, and those assumptions are only available for scrutiny if
they are written down. Stating a fitted $R^2$ without showing where the
underlying formula comes from invites the reader to take the assumptions on
faith; deriving the formula from stated primitives lets the reader see
exactly what would have to be true of the world for the fit to mean what the
author says it means --- and, just as importantly, lets the reader judge for
themselves whether each assumption is plausible, and reject the ones that are
not. That is the spirit in which the rest of this section proceeds.

\subsection{What has to be explained}

Strip away the historical narrative and the book's empirical exercise is this:
across roughly a dozen distinct kinship types --- spouses, parent-child,
full siblings, aunt/uncle-niece/nephew, grandparent-grandchild, first
cousins, second cousins, and so on --- Clark observes a correlation
$\hat\rho_k$ in some status outcome, for $k=1,\dots,K$. The model supplies a
predicted value for each,
\begin{equation}
E(\hat\rho_k) = f_k(\theta), \qquad \theta = (h^2, r, m),
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
and the question is whether a single, low-dimensional $\theta$ can make all
$K$ of these predictions line up with the data at once. Table~\ref{tab:notation}
below collects the $f_k$'s derived in this section. The book reports that they
do line up remarkably well (Table A1, $R^2>0.97$ for several outcomes), and
treats this as strong evidence that the underlying process generating status
resemblance among relatives is additive genetic transmission with assortative
mating. Whether that inference is warranted depends entirely on what has to be
assumed to write down the $f_k$'s in the first place, and on how many of the
three symbols in $\theta$ are actually free. Both turn out to be more
demanding, and more restrictive, than the book's presentation suggests.

\subsection{The central assumption: genes as the sole channel of resemblance}

The single most consequential assumption in the model is easy to state and
easy to miss: \emph{the only reason any two relatives resemble each other is
that they share genes.} Following the way Arthur Goldberger and others in the
biometric tradition set the model up, write each person's standardized
phenotype as a weighted sum of an additive genotypic value and an independent
environmental deviation,
\begin{equation}
y = a\,G + e\,E, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(G,E)=0,
\label{eq:model-raw}
\end{equation}
with $a^2+e^2=1$ so that $\operatorname{Var}(y)=1$; below I write $h^2\equiv a^2$ for
heritability and absorb the loadings so that $\operatorname{Var}(G)=h^2$,
$\operatorname{Var}(E)=1-h^2$, recovering the more compact $y=G+E$ used from here on. For
any two relatives 1 and 2 whose genotypes have correlation $\operatorname{Corr}(G_1,G_2)$,
and whose environments are assumed uncorrelated with each other and with the
other's genotype, the model implies
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(y_1,y_2) = h^2\operatorname{Corr}(G_1,G_2).
\label{eq:sole-channel}
\end{equation}
Every one of the model's predictions --- for siblings, cousins, grandparents,
whatever the relationship --- is an application of equation~\eqref{eq:sole-channel}
with a different value of $\operatorname{Corr}(G_1,G_2)$ plugged in. Nothing else is
allowed to generate resemblance: not a shared home, not shared schooling, not
shared neighborhood, not parenting style, not culture. As I put it in my own
correspondence on this project, this is every bit as implausible, on its face,
as a model that ignores genes entirely --- it simply replaces one extreme
assumption (environment only) with the opposite extreme (genes only), and the
book never argues for the extreme it picks beyond noting that the fitted model
happens to track the data.

This is not merely a theoretical worry. My own referee report on the
underlying 2023 PNAS paper \citep{Clark2023} made the same point directly:
the model as specified predicts that relatives reared together and relatives
reared apart should be equally similar, since only shared genes matter and
rearing arrangement is irrelevant to shared genotype. That prediction is
false. Sibling and twin correlations are consistently higher for pairs reared
together than for pairs reared apart \citep{CesariniVisscher2017}, and
genetically unrelated adoptees reared in the same home show some resemblance
in exactly the kinds of social-status outcomes this book studies. Both facts
require some real channel of environmental resemblance among relatives who
are not spouses --- precisely the channel equation~\eqref{eq:sole-channel}
sets to zero by assumption, not by test.

\subsection{Assortative mating and its consequence for spouses}

Mating is assortative on the \emph{phenotype}: spouses' $y$ values have
correlation
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Corr}(y_h, y_w) = r,
\end{equation}
where $h,w$ index husband and wife. Nothing is assumed about how spouses come
to resemble each other on $y$ --- the model is agnostic about mechanism --- but
everything that follows is a mechanical consequence of assortment operating on
the \emph{whole} phenotype rather than on genotype alone. This is Clark's
simplified, dominance-free version of Fisher's model; it is the version whose
moment conditions generate the book's Table A1 and the decay curve
$\rho_n = h^2\left(\frac{1+m}{2}\right)^n$ fit to collateral relatives $n$
steps removed.

\subsection{What phenotypic assortment implies for spouses' genes and environments}

Because $G$ and $E$ are the only two components of $y$, and because $y$ is what
spouses match on, both components are dragged into correlation across spouses
--- not just the genetic one. To see this, regress each spouse's genetic
component on their own phenotype. Since $\operatorname{Cov}(G,y)=\operatorname{Var}(G)=h^2$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(y)=1$, the regression coefficient is $h^2$ itself:
\begin{equation}
G_h = h^2 y_h + u_h, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(u_h) = h^2(1-h^2),
\end{equation}
with $u_h$ uncorrelated with $y_h$ by construction, and an identical equation
for the wife. Maintaining the assumption that every cross-spouse correlation
runs \emph{only} through the phenotype-matching channel --- i.e.\ that the
residuals $u_h,u_w$ are uncorrelated with each other and with the spouse's
phenotype --- gives
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(G_h,G_w) = h^4\operatorname{Cov}(y_h,y_w) = h^4 r,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\operatorname{Corr}(G_h,G_w) = h^2 r \equiv m.
\label{eq:m-def}
\end{equation}
This recovers Clark's identity $m=h^2r$: the genotypic correlation between
spouses is not a free parameter, it is a forced arithmetic consequence of
assorting on the phenotype with correlation $r$ and heritability $h^2$.

The same regression logic, applied to $E$ instead of $G$ (regression
coefficient $1-h^2$ rather than $h^2$, since $\operatorname{Cov}(E,y)=\operatorname{Var}(E)=1-h^2$), gives
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(E_h,E_w) = (1-h^2)^2 r, \qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(G_h,E_w) = h^2(1-h^2)r.
\label{eq:ge-cross}
\end{equation}
This is the point Arthur Goldberger raised about Fisher's model, and which I
raised independently in my own referee report on the 2023 PNAS paper \citep{Clark2023}: phenotypic
assortment does not merely correlate spouses' genes, it \emph{also} correlates
their environments, and it correlates one spouse's genes with the other's
environment. Every other pair of relatives in the model --- siblings, cousins,
a person and their own best man --- is assumed to have \emph{zero}
environmental resemblance and zero gene-environment cross-correlation. There is
no substantive theory in the book of why marriage, specifically, is the one
relationship that generates environmental resemblance while every other
family tie generates none. As I put it in my own correspondence on this paper:
it is hard to imagine a realistic real-world process that produces
environmental resemblance between spouses but not between siblings.

This is not a decorative objection. It is doing real work, as the next two
derivations show.

\subsection{Transmission, and the sibling correlation}

A child's genotype is the average of the two parents' genotypes plus an
independent Mendelian segregation term $s$,
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{child}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(G_m + G_f) + s, \qquad s \perp G_m, G_f, E_{\text{anyone}},
\end{equation}
with $\operatorname{Var}(s)$ pinned down by the equilibrium requirement
$\operatorname{Var}(G_{\text{child}})=h^2$. Two siblings share both parents but draw
independent segregation terms $s_1, s_2$. Under the model's blanket assumption
that non-spousal relatives share no environmental correlation and no
gene-environment cross-correlation, the sibling phenotypic covariance reduces
to the genetic term alone:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(y_1,y_2) = \operatorname{Cov}(G_1,G_2)
= \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(G_m+G_f)\right)
= \tfrac{1}{4}\big[h^2+h^2+2mh^2\big]
= \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(1+m).
\label{eq:sib}
\end{equation}
This is the familiar formula behind the collateral-relative decay curve: sibling
resemblance runs entirely through the genotypic spousal correlation $m$.

\subsection{The parent-offspring correlation}

Now compute the covariance between a child and one parent, say the father.
Because the child's own environment $E_{\text{child}}$ is assumed independent
of everything about the parents,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(y_{\text{child}}, y_f) = \operatorname{Cov}(G_{\text{child}}, G_f) + \operatorname{Cov}(G_{\text{child}}, E_f).
\end{equation}
The first term is exactly the genetic term computed above for siblings,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(G_{\text{child}}, G_f) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\operatorname{Cov}(G_m,G_f)+\operatorname{Var}(G_f)\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(1+m).
\end{equation}
But there is a second term that siblings do not have: the child inherits half
its genes from the mother, and the mother's genes are cross-correlated with the
\emph{father's environment} through the marriage --- exactly the term derived
in equation~\eqref{eq:ge-cross}:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(G_{\text{child}}, E_f) = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Cov}(G_m, E_f) = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(1-h^2)r.
\end{equation}
Adding the two terms and substituting $m=h^2r$:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Cov}(y_{\text{child}},y_f)
&= \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(1+m) + \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(1-h^2)r \notag\\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}h^2\Big[1 + h^2 r + (1-h^2)r\Big] \notag\\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}h^2\big[1+r\big].
\label{eq:po}
\end{align}
So $\rho_{PO} = h^2(1+r)/2$: the parent-offspring correlation runs through
$r$, not $m$. This is worth pausing on. It is precisely the spousal
gene-environment cross-term that Goldberger flagged as implausible --- the
term with no analogue anywhere else in the model --- that is mathematically
responsible for parent-offspring resemblance exceeding sibling resemblance.
The "curious exception" for spouses is not a side remark; it is load-bearing.

\subsection{The decisive test: $\rho_{PO}$ vs.\ $\rho_S$}

Comparing equations~\eqref{eq:sib} and~\eqref{eq:po}, and using $m=h^2r$
again:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{PO} - \rho_S = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2(r-m) = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2 r(1-h^2) \; > \; 0
\qquad\text{whenever } 0<h^2<1.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The model does not merely permit parent-offspring correlation to exceed
sibling correlation --- it \emph{requires} it, by a specific, computable
amount, given only the two parameters Clark himself estimates.

Plug in Clark's own reported values, $r\approx0.8$ and $m\approx0.6$, which
imply $h^2=m/r=0.75$. Equation~\eqref{eq:gap} predicts
\begin{equation}
\rho_{PO}-\rho_S \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}(0.75)(0.8-0.6) \;=\; 0.075.
\end{equation}
The book's own Table 4.5 reports raw sibling and parent-child (``Full
Sibling'' and ``Child'') correlations in occupational status for people born
1780--1859 of $0.584$ and $0.585$ respectively --- a gap of $0.001$, smaller
than the model's own prediction by roughly two orders of magnitude. This is not a subtle specification-testing exercise
requiring auxiliary assumptions --- it is arithmetic, performed entirely with
numbers the author reports himself. Clark comes close to seeing the problem
(p.~61: parity between the two correlations "would only be predicted\ldots if
parents were actually matching on the genotype itself") but never computes the
size of the discrepancy his own model predicts, and does not revise the model,
or his confidence in its fit, in light of it.

\subsection{Two parameters, not three: the Jinks-and-Eaves precedent}

Equation~\eqref{eq:m-def} is the source of a second, closely related problem.
Because $m=h^2r$ is a forced identity rather than an independent empirical
fact, the model has exactly two free structural parameters --- one may
parameterize it as $(h^2,r)$ or as $(h^2,m)$, but not as all three
$(h^2,r,m)$ simultaneously without imposing the constraint that links them.

This is not a new observation; it is the identical error \citet{Goldberger1978}
diagnosed in Jinks and Eaves's reanalysis of the British and American
IQ-kinship data. Jinks and Eaves treated the genotypic spousal correlation $A$
as a free parameter, in violation of Fisher's own requirement that
$A=c_1c_2m$, and their fitted estimates duly violated it (for the British data
set, $c_1c_2m=.22$ against a fitted $A=.47$). When Goldberger refit the model
with the constraint properly imposed, the fit measurably worsened: $\chi^2$
rose from $9.05$ (9 d.f., $A$ free) to $13.65$ (10 d.f., constraint imposed)
--- an increment of $4.60$ on one degree of freedom, significant at the 5\%
level, which Goldberger read as "a piece of evidence against the classical
model" even though the constrained model still fit acceptably well in absolute
terms.

Clark's book \citep{Clark2026} (Technical Appendix, eqs.\ A1--A3) fits $m$ from the
collateral-relative decay curve and $r$ from marriage records as two
essentially separate exercises, and at no point jointly imposes the two-
parameter constraint across all relationship types --- parent-offspring
included --- simultaneously. This is the same practice Goldberger's
re-analysis showed to be non-innocuous three decades before the underlying
PNAS paper was written, and it is exactly the point I raised in my referee
report on that paper: ``if sorting is on phenotype, the model \ldots\ implies
$m=rh^2$ and hence the moment conditions can (and should, as far as I can
tell) be expressed in terms of only two parameters \ldots\ I do not understand
the justification for not imposing the constraint in the estimation (which
would mean that two free parameters are estimated instead of three) \ldots\
the paper's current estimation approach causes the model fit to be
artificially inflated.''

Clark's response was that spouses may be assorting on some ``wider
phenotype'' than the one being modeled directly --- a coherent enough claim
about the assortment \emph{process}, but one that leaves unanswered whether
the formulas in Table A1 (which are derived under phenotypic homogamy on the
modeled trait itself) remain valid at all once that broader, unmodeled
phenotype is doing the assorting. My second-round report pressed exactly this
point and did not receive a resolution: ``It is still not entirely clear to
me if the author is estimating the model with the constraint imposed or not
(i.e.\ with two or three free parameters?) \ldots\ The alternative model may
also imply some restrictions on how $m$, $r$, and $h^2$ are related. If so,
these restrictions should be imposed in estimation.'' The book carries this
unresolved status forward unchanged, and richer models that relax the
sole-genetic-channel assumption of Section~2.2 --- surveyed, for instance, by
\citet{OttoEtAl1995} --- are not engaged with at all.

\section{The Identification Problem}

\subsection{Good fit does not imply unbiased estimates}

Set aside, for a moment, whether Clark's fit claims are even correct on their
own terms. There is a prior, more basic problem: good fit would not settle
the matter even if it held up perfectly. A correctly specified model should
generally be expected to fit the data well and to yield unbiased parameter
estimates. The converse does not hold. A misspecified model can fit
observed correlations closely while its parameter estimates mean nothing
close to what the author claims they mean, because the fitted parameters are
absorbing the specification error along with the genuine signal. Treating
close agreement between predicted and observed correlations as validation of
the assumptions used to derive the predictions --- rather than as one
necessary, but far from sufficient, condition for those assumptions to be
right --- is a fallacy with a long and specifically relevant history in
exactly this empirical literature.

\subsection{The 1970s precedent}

In the mid-1970s two rival groups --- Jinks and Eaves at Birmingham, and Rao,
Morton, and Yee at Honolulu --- fit competing biometric models of essentially
the same kind to overlapping British and American IQ kinship-correlation
data, and each group reported an excellent fit. Goldberger's (1978)
re-examination of both efforts, transcribed in full in the companion file
\texttt{Goldberger\_1978\_ModelsAndMethods.tex} included in this repository,
is instructive on two counts.

First, the two models, despite each fitting its own data well, produced
substantively incompatible conclusions. Fit to overlapping American data,
Rao and Morton's own estimate of narrow heritability of IQ among children was
$.69$, against Jinks and Eaves's $.34$ --- roughly a twofold disagreement on
the same underlying quantity, from ostensibly similar exercises. The two
groups also disagreed about the sign of the adult-versus-childhood pattern:
Rao and Morton's estimate of broad heritability among American adults was
$.30$, falling relative to children, while Jinks and Eaves's corresponding
estimate was $.68$, unchanged from their childhood estimate. A reader told
only that both models ``fit the data well'' would have no way to know that
the two exercises disagreed this sharply about what the data implied.

Second, and more pointed, Goldberger showed algebraically that the Honolulu
group's own model was not even \emph{identified}: ``the model was
indeterminate, that is `non-estimable,' that is `not identified': even if the
population values of all 11 kinship correlations were known, unique values of
the [model's] parameters could not be obtained.'' This was true despite the
model's reported fit being, in Goldberger's words, ``clearly very good.'' A
model can fit the available correlations closely while having no unique
solution for its own parameters --- the appearance of a well-fitting,
informative estimate can coexist with there being, mathematically, no
information about the parameters of interest in the data at all.

\subsection{The same pattern, on Clark's own data}

This is not merely a historical curiosity; Section 2 already showed a version
of it playing out in Clark's own numbers. Table~\ref{tab:table45} reproduces

Because a common, phenotype-specific attenuation factor is assumed to scale
every raw correlation for a given outcome by the same constant (this is the
premise of Clark's own ratio method for correcting these raw numbers), that
factor cancels in the comparison of $\hat\rho_{PO}$ to $\hat\rho_S$ for a
given outcome and period, and the sign of the gap can be read directly off
the raw table without needing Clark's separately estimated $h^2,r,m$ at all.
Equation~\eqref{eq:gap} shows the model requires $\rho_{PO}>\rho_S$
\emph{strictly}, for any heritability less than $1$. For occupational status
the raw gap is at least the right sign, if two orders of magnitude too small
relative to the model's own prediction under Clark's reported parameters.
For higher education, in both periods, the sign is wrong: siblings are more
alike than parent and child, which the model forbids outright regardless of
what values $h^2$, $r$, and $m$ take. This is a sharper and more direct
disconfirmation than a goodness-of-fit statistic computed over many outcomes
at once could ever reveal, precisely because it does not depend on any
particular parameter estimate.

\subsection{External evidence the book does not engage}

A further way to adjudicate between observationally similar models --- short
of the kind of kinship-correlation moment-matching this section has just
shown to be unreliable on its own --- is to bring in evidence external to the
correlations being fit. The clearest example concerns the value of $m$ (the
genotypic spousal correlation) that Clark treats as consistent with modern
genetic evidence. Genome-wide association study estimates of the kind Clark
cites for parental genetic similarity are estimates of \emph{population}
effects, not \emph{direct} (causal) effects, and a substantial recent
literature shows these need not be proportional to one another
\citep{YoungEtAl2019,YoungEtAl2022}. Treating an $m\approx0.6$ derived from
population-genetic estimates as evidence about \emph{direct} genetic
transmission of status --- the quantity the book's causal claims actually
rest on --- is not obviously licensed once that distinction is taken
seriously. Related work on ``genetic nurture,'' in which a parent's genotype
affects a child's outcomes through the environment the parent provides rather
than through the genes transmitted to the child \citep{KongEtAl2018}, is
exactly the kind of indirect, non-additive-transmission channel that the
sole-genetic-channel assumption of Section~2.2 rules out by fiat.

The book's own Chapter 6 takes up genetic nurture directly and concludes that
recent attempts to demonstrate its importance fail, arguing that twin and
adoption studies would already have detected such effects if they were large.
This does not engage the population-versus-direct-effects distinction above,
which is a separate concern from the twin/adoption evidence Chapter 6
addresses; nor does it engage \citet{KongEtAl2018}'s design, which detects
genetic nurture using a method (non-transmitted parental alleles) that does
not rely on the twin/adoption comparisons Chapter 6 critiques. I raised a
version of this gap directly in my referee report on the underlying PNAS
paper, where Clark's response offered an unpublished personal simulation
arguing that direct-effects-only models with sufficient assortment could
reproduce apparent genetic-nurture patterns; that simulation was not, and has
not since been, made available for independent scrutiny.

A related, longer-standing literature makes the same kind of point about
within-family comparisons more generally. MZ-twin-difference designs, widely
used to estimate the return to schooling on the logic that identical twins
share both genotype and family background \citep{Griliches1979}, are only as
good as the assumption that within-pair schooling differences are unrelated
to individual ability \citep{Griliches1977,BoundSolon1999,Neumark1999}.
Directly testing this assumption, \citet{SandewallEtAl2014} find that
within-pair differences in adolescent IQ predict within-pair differences in
educational attainment among monozygotic twins, and that controlling for IQ
reduces the within-pair estimated return to schooling by roughly 15\%. If
ability differences of exactly this kind survive even within monozygotic
twin pairs, treating any specific empirical design --- Clark's collateral-
relative decay curve included --- as if it cleanly isolates a single
additive genetic channel, free of confounding by within-family differences
in unmeasured ability, is not something that can simply be assumed.

\section{What ``Genetic'' Does Not Mean}

\subsection{Genes fixed is not the same as genes' effects fixed}

Even granting Clark's estimates at face value, the inferential leap from
``status is highly heritable'' to ``society has little room to change social
outcomes'' does not go through. ``Genetic,'' in the variance-decomposition
sense used throughout this model, means only that an outcome is statistically
associated with genotype; it says nothing about the causal pathway by which
that association arises, and that pathway can run through channels ordinary
usage would call environmental and modifiable. Body-mass index is highly
heritable by exactly this kind of estimate, yet the pathway from genotype to
body mass runs substantially through food choice and health behavior --- both
manifestly alterable. The point, due to Christopher Jencks, is that a
genotype being fixed (nobody chooses their own genes) is a completely
different claim from a genotype's \emph{effects} being fixed or immutable.
Reared-together versus reared-apart comparisons already discussed in
Section~2.2 make the same point from the environmental side: if shared
environment truly contributed nothing, as the model's sole-genetic-channel
assumption requires for every relationship except marriage, those comparisons
should show no difference, and they do.

\subsection{Heritable is not the same as unchangeable}

The book goes further than the general inference above and states the
stronger claim directly: ``The second implication of direct genetic
transmission is that social outcomes will change \ldots\ only when the
genetic endowment of the society changes'' (p.~10). This is not a defensible
reading of what heritability estimates license, and standard examples from
outside the social sciences make the point sharply. Phenylketonuria is,
absent intervention, about as close to a purely genetic determinant of
outcome as exists in human biology --- yet it is entirely preventable by a
change in diet, an intervention that has nothing to do with the genetic
endowment of anyone. Myopia is highly heritable and is fully correctable with
eyeglasses. Closer to the social outcomes this book studies, a literacy
program that teaches a population to read can change welfare enormously
regardless of the heritability of whatever cognitive or social trait
``literacy'' loads onto --- heritability describes the sources of variation
\emph{within} a given environmental range, not a ceiling on what a new
environmental intervention outside that range can do.

\subsection{Ordinal mobility is not the only thing policy can be for}

There is also a narrower, more specific problem with how the book gets from
its estimates to its policy conclusions. The Introduction's own overview of Part III states:
``The first
implication of the importance of direct genetic transmission of social
outcomes is that modern societies spend too much on education \ldots\ What
drives much of this expenditure is a belief that boosting years of education
by less advantaged students increases social mobility rates. Yet despite a
substantial narrowing of the gap in years of education between high and low
status students 1851--2022, social mobility rates are unchanged'' (p.~9). The
argument silently assumes that the \emph{only} legitimate justification for
education spending is changing relative, ordinal mobility --- moving people
up and down the status distribution relative to one another, which is what
the book's intergenerational transmission coefficients measure. But a policy
can be worth its cost by raising everyone's \emph{absolute} outcomes even if
it leaves the ordinal ranking, and the estimated transmission coefficients,
completely untouched: a literacy program that lifts the reading level of an
entire cohort improves welfare regardless of whether the same families end up
in the same relative positions afterward. Evidence that an intervention did
not change the intergenerational correlation is evidence about mobility; it
is not, by itself, evidence about whether the intervention was worthwhile.

The specific empirical claim that follows --- ``studies of the effect of
increases in compulsory years of education for less able students in
1918--22, 1947, and 1972 find no effect on employment, income, or health''
(p.~9) --- is also not uncontested.

Nor is the birth-order test in Part II as distinctively confirmatory of the
genetic model as the book presents it: prior labor-economics literature
already finds birth-order effects on educational and economic outcomes to be
real but small \citep{BlackDevereuxSalvanes2005}, so a modest or null
birth-order effect in the FOE data is also compatible with ordinary
environmental accounts of status transmission, not only with the additive
genetic model.
